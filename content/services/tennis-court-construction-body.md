## Tennis Court Problems That Begin With the Wrong Construction Approach

Tennis involves quick changes in direction, repeated ball impacts, and movement across a large playing area. Even small problems with the court surface can affect how the game feels.

Outdoor courts also face weather conditions that may lead to wear over time.

Common issues include:

- **Uneven ball bounce:** Surface dips, raised areas, or damaged pavement can cause the ball to react differently during rallies.
- **Cracks in the court base:** Ground movement, temperature changes, and aging materials may contribute to cracking in asphalt or concrete.
- **Poor water runoff:** Improper grading can leave puddles that interrupt play and contribute to surface deterioration.
- **Limited space behind baselines:** Courts without enough surrounding clearance can make it difficult for players to reach deep shots.
- **Faded lines and coatings:** Sun exposure and regular use may cause playing markings and surface colors to wear.
- **Damaged fencing:** Loose or worn perimeter fencing can affect ball containment and the surrounding recreational area.
- **Unsuitable surface materials:** A court system that doesn't match the intended use or site conditions may require changes or additional maintenance.
- **Incorrect court orientation:** Poor positioning can expose players to direct sunlight during frequently scheduled playing hours.
- **Inconsistent surface traction:** Unsuitable textures, worn coatings, and accumulated debris may affect footing during quick movements.
- **Unstable tennis net posts:** Improperly supported posts can affect net tension, alignment, and playing conditions.
- **Inadequate foundation preparation:** Poorly compacted soil or unsuitable base materials can contribute to settlement and recurring pavement damage.
- **Improper court lighting:** Uneven illumination, glare, or poorly positioned fixtures can interfere with visibility during evening games.

## Selecting the Best Tennis Court Orientation

Court orientation influences sunlight exposure, visibility, wind conditions, and player comfort.

A tennis court should be positioned according to the property's layout rather than simply placed wherever a rectangular area appears to fit.

### North-South Court Orientation

Outdoor tennis courts are commonly oriented in a generally north-south direction where practical.

This can help reduce the impact of low-angle sunlight shining directly into players' eyes during morning and evening matches.

However, the ideal alignment also depends on latitude, local conditions, surrounding structures, and anticipated playing hours.

### Wind Exposure

Wind can affect the direction and speed of tennis balls during serves, volleys, and groundstrokes.

Properties in open areas, near foothills, or on elevated terrain may experience different wind conditions from enclosed residential yards.

Court orientation and suitable windscreen options can help address certain wind-related concerns.

### Nearby Trees and Buildings

Existing trees may provide shade, but they can also introduce leaves, debris, root-related concerns, and uneven shadows across the playing surface.

Buildings, walls, and other structures can affect available space, wind patterns, and court access.

These features should be considered before selecting the final court position.

### Neighboring Property Considerations

Residential tennis courts should also account for nearby homes, property boundaries, lighting exposure, and recreational noise.

This is especially important in master-planned communities and properties governed by HOA requirements.

A thoughtfully positioned court can improve everyday usability while minimizing unnecessary conflicts with surrounding outdoor areas.

## What Goes Underneath a Professionally Built Tennis Court?

The visible playing surface represents only one part of tennis court construction.

A long-lasting hardcourt depends on the condition of the underlying soil, supporting base, foundation, and drainage system.

### Soil Evaluation and Subgrade Preparation

Before construction begins, the proposed court area should be evaluated for soil conditions, existing slopes, drainage patterns, and previous construction activity.

Unsuitable fill, organic materials, unstable ground, and existing deteriorated pavement may need to be addressed.

Certain properties may require additional soil testing or engineering to determine appropriate foundation specifications.

### Excavation and Grading

Excavation helps prepare the property for the planned construction layers.

Grading establishes the elevations required for the court foundation, surrounding clearance, and drainage system.

Unlike a completely level indoor playing surface, an outdoor hardcourt generally needs a slight, consistent slope for water runoff.

### Compacted Aggregate Base

A properly prepared aggregate base helps establish consistent support beneath concrete or asphalt.

The necessary material, depth, and compaction depend on the existing ground conditions and selected court system.

Poorly prepared aggregate can contribute to settlement, cracking, and surface irregularities.

### Concrete or Asphalt Foundation

The structural pavement provides support for the finished tennis court surface.

Foundation specifications should account for site conditions, anticipated use, drainage requirements, and compatibility with the selected athletic coating.

### Athletic Surface Preparation

Before acrylic coatings or modular flooring are installed, the foundation should meet the applicable requirements for curing, moisture, cleanliness, surface consistency, and material compatibility.

Proper preparation helps support a more uniform playing surface and reduces avoidable installation problems.

## Concrete vs. Asphalt Tennis Court Construction

Concrete and asphalt are widely used as foundations for outdoor hardcourt tennis.

Each material has different construction characteristics, and the most appropriate choice depends on the property and proposed surface system.

| Construction consideration | Concrete tennis court | Asphalt tennis court |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural characteristics | Rigid slab | More flexible pavement system |
| Base requirements | Stable, appropriately prepared supporting material | Compacted aggregate and suitable subgrade |
| Construction considerations | Reinforcement, curing, and joint design | Pavement thickness, compaction, and surface preparation |
| Common surface finishes | Acrylic and compatible modular systems | Acrylic and other compatible athletic finishes |
| Potential deterioration | Cracking, settlement, and joint movement | Cracking, oxidation, and pavement deformation |
| Weather considerations | Moisture management and temperature-related movement | Heat exposure, moisture, and seasonal pavement changes |
| Maintenance | Depends on the slab and finished athletic surface | Depends on pavement condition and surface system |

### Conventional Concrete Tennis Courts

Concrete can provide a rigid and stable foundation for a properly designed tennis court.

Slab thickness, reinforcement, joint placement, and supporting materials should reflect actual site conditions and project requirements.

Control joints and structural movement also need to be considered when selecting the finished athletic surface.

### Post-Tensioned Concrete Considerations

Some tennis court projects use engineered post-tensioned concrete foundations.

This construction method uses tensioned steel tendons to introduce compression into the slab, which may help control certain types of cracking.

Post-tensioned construction requires specialized engineering and installation procedures.

It does not eliminate every risk associated with ground movement, inadequate drainage, or unsuitable soil conditions.

### Asphalt Tennis Court Bases

Asphalt provides another foundation option for outdoor tennis courts.

A properly installed asphalt base can support compatible acrylic athletic coatings and other suitable finishes.

The underlying aggregate, pavement compaction, surface consistency, and drainage arrangements all contribute to long-term performance.

Neither concrete nor asphalt is automatically the best choice for every tennis court.

## Tennis Court Drainage and Surface Slope

Effective water management is essential for outdoor tennis courts.

Poor drainage can leave standing water, contribute to coating deterioration, and affect the supporting pavement over time.

### Establishing the Correct Playing Surface Slope

Outdoor acrylic hardcourts generally require a slight, consistent slope so rainfall can move off the playing surface.

Depending on the selected court design and applicable specifications, slopes of approximately 0.5% to 1% may be appropriate for certain hardcourt systems.

The final slope should be determined according to relevant tennis court construction standards and site conditions.

Excessive or inconsistent slopes may affect play, while insufficient drainage can contribute to persistent puddles.

### Perimeter Drainage Planning

Water flowing from nearby roofs, patios, lawns, driveways, and landscaped areas should not enter the court uncontrollably.

Surrounding grades and drainage features can help direct runoff toward appropriate outlets.

### Protecting the Court Foundation

Moisture accumulating beneath or around the foundation may affect certain soil conditions and contribute to pavement movement.

Appropriate aggregate materials, compaction, grading, and drainage can help reduce these risks.

### Identifying Low Spots on Existing Courts

Repeated water accumulation in the same area may indicate surface irregularities, settlement, or drainage problems.

These conditions should be evaluated before resurfacing an existing court.

A new acrylic coating cannot independently correct significant foundation movement or substantial low spots.

An existing tennis court may remain structurally usable even after its finished surface begins to deteriorate.

- Worn or peeling acrylic coatings
- Visible pavement cracks
- Uneven ball bounce
- Persistent standing water
- Reduced surface traction
- Damaged net posts
- Loose fencing
- Surface discoloration

### Tennis Court Crack Repair

Certain localized cracks may be suitable for compatible repair methods.

## How to Maintain Your Tennis Court After Construction

Proper maintenance helps preserve playing surface consistency, equipment functionality, and the overall appearance of the facility.

Maintenance requirements depend on the installed surface, local climate, and frequency of play.

### Remove Leaves, Dust, and Surface Debris

Outdoor tennis courts should be kept free of loose debris that can affect traction and appearance.

Cleaning methods should be appropriate for the selected surface system.

Avoid aggressive cleaning equipment that could damage acrylic coatings or other athletic flooring materials.

### Monitor Cracks and Surface Wear

Inspect the court periodically for visible cracks, worn coatings, peeling finishes, and uneven areas.

Addressing developing concerns can help identify whether localized repairs or resurfacing are appropriate.

### Check Net Tension and Equipment

Tennis nets, center straps, posts, anchors, and supporting equipment should be inspected periodically.

Damaged or unstable components should be addressed before regular use continues.

### Inspect Perimeter Fencing and Windscreens

Loose fencing, deteriorated connections, and damaged windscreen attachments can affect the surrounding recreational area.

Check these components after severe weather and during routine facility inspections.

### Maintain Suitable Drainage

Observe how the court drains after heavy rainfall or snowmelt.

Recurring puddles, perimeter erosion, and displaced materials may indicate drainage concerns that require additional evaluation.

### Schedule Resurfacing According to Court Condition

Acrylic tennis courts commonly require resurfacing after several years of regular use.

The appropriate timing depends on surface wear, weather exposure, maintenance history, and the condition of the supporting pavement.

Commercial facilities with frequent player traffic may have different resurfacing requirements from private residential courts.

| Maintenance activity | Suggested timing |
| --- | --- |
| Remove loose debris | Before play and as needed |
| Clean the playing surface | According to usage and manufacturer guidance |
| Inspect visible cracks | Periodically and after severe weather |
| Check tennis net components | During routine equipment inspections |
| Review fencing and windscreens | Periodically |
| Evaluate drainage | After significant rainfall or snowmelt |
| Assess resurfacing requirements | When the surface shows noticeable deterioration |

A suitable maintenance routine can support the long-term condition of your tennis court, although it cannot prevent every type of material deterioration.
